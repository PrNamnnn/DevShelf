## What it is

Regression evaluation measures how far predictions fall from true numeric outcomes. The statistics book covers diagnostics of a fitted model, and this lesson covers comparing predictive models.

## Why it exists

The right metric depends on what errors cost, and an averaged score can conceal poor performance in key segments.

## How it works

Use MAE when cost grows linearly, RMSE when large errors matter more, and quantile loss for asymmetric costs. Always compare against a baseline such as predicting the mean or last value. Examine errors by segment, time and magnitude. Match CV to data: time-based for forecasts and group-based for repeated entities.

## Intuition and formula

<Formula>R² = 1 − Σ(y − ŷ)² / Σ(y − ȳ)²</Formula>

On held-out data, R² can be negative when a model is worse than predicting the mean.

## Worked example

Predictions of 10, 20 and 30 against actuals of 12, 18 and 33 yield an MAE of 2.33 and an RMSE of 2.38. Predicting with a much worse model can produce R² below 0.

## Python

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error, mean_squared_error, r2_score

y = np.array([12.0, 18.0, 33.0])
pred = np.array([10.0, 20.0, 30.0])

assert round(mean_absolute_error(y, pred), 2) == 2.33
assert round(np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(y, pred)), 2) == 2.38
assert r2_score(y, np.array([33.0, 12.0, 18.0])) < 0
```

## In industry

Demand models report weighted errors by product value, and pricing models monitor bias, the mean signed error, separately.

## Assumptions and limitations

Single-number summaries hide bias across segments. MAPE breaks near zero.

## Common mistakes

- Treating R² as universally comparable.
- Random CV on time-indexed data.
- Ignoring systematic over-prediction.
- Evaluating in transformed units only.

## Alternatives

Prediction intervals and pinball loss evaluate uncertainty.

## Interview questions

<Question q="Can R² be negative?">Yes, on held-out data, if the predictions are worse than the mean.</Question>

<Question q="MAE or RMSE?">MAE for linear costs. RMSE when large errors are especially bad.</Question>
