## What it is

A **population** is the full group you want to draw conclusions about. A **sample** is the subset you measured. A **parameter** describes the population (μ, σ, p). A **statistic** is computed from a sample (x̄, s, p̂) and estimates a parameter.

## Why it exists

Measuring everyone is often impossible or wasteful. A well-drawn sample of a few thousand can estimate a national rate precisely, but a badly drawn sample of millions can be confidently wrong, because a larger sample shrinks random error and leaves bias untouched.

## How it works

Probability sampling gives each unit a known chance of selection:

| Method | How | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple random | Every unit equally likely | A complete list exists |
| Systematic | Every kth unit | Ordered lists with no matching cycle |
| Stratified | Random within subgroups | Guaranteeing small segments |
| Cluster | Randomly choose whole groups | Costly travel or naturally grouped units |

Convenience, volunteer and quota samples are non-probability methods, so their error can't be quantified the same way.

Common biases:

- **Selection bias**: the way units enter the data relates to the outcome.
- **Survivorship bias**: only those who lasted are visible.
- **Non-response bias**: those who answer differ from those who don't.
- **Measurement bias**: the instrument is systematically off.

**Observational** studies record what happens. **Experiments** assign treatment randomly, which balances known and unknown confounders on average and supports causal claims.

## Worked example

A company emails a satisfaction survey to all customers. 4% respond, averaging 4.6 out of 5, and the team reports "customers are delighted". But unhappy customers who already churned weren't emailed (survivorship), and those with strong feelings answer more readily (non-response). A better design randomly samples customers, including recently churned ones, follows up with non-responders, and compares responders with the population on known fields like tenure.

## Python

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
users = pd.DataFrame({
    "segment": ["enterprise"] * 200 + ["self-serve"] * 9_800,
    "spend": np.r_[rng.normal(5_000, 500, 200), rng.normal(50, 10, 9_800)],
})
stratified = users.groupby("segment").sample(frac=0.05, random_state=42)

counts = stratified["segment"].value_counts()
assert counts["enterprise"] == 10 and counts["self-serve"] == 490
```

## SQL

```sql
SELECT segment, count(*) AS sampled
FROM (
  SELECT segment,
         row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY segment ORDER BY md5(user_id::text)) AS rn,
         count(*) OVER (PARTITION BY segment) AS total
  FROM (SELECT g AS user_id, CASE WHEN g <= 100 THEN 'a' ELSE 'b' END AS segment
        FROM generate_series(1, 1000) AS g) AS users
) AS ranked
WHERE rn <= total * 0.1
GROUP BY segment
ORDER BY segment;
-- rows: [{"segment":"a","sampled":10},{"segment":"b","sampled":90}]
```

Hashing an ID makes selection repeatable, which plain `random()` isn't.

## In industry

Product telemetry can look like a census while only covering logged-in users on instrumented app versions. Experimentation platforms hash user IDs for stable assignment, and fraud or quality review teams often stratify so rare cases are represented.

## Assumptions and limitations

Sampling theory assumes a frame that covers the population. If the list omits people, randomization can't recover them. Standard formulas also assume independence, so clustered samples need design-aware standard errors.

## Common mistakes

- Using a very large sample as evidence that bias doesn't matter.
- Analyzing only active users when studying why people leave.
- Treating web analytics sessions as independent when many belong to one user.
- Inferring beyond the population sampled.

## Alternatives

When randomization is impossible, quasi-experimental designs such as difference-in-differences or regression discontinuity approximate it under stated assumptions. Survey weighting and post-stratification can reduce known imbalance, but they can't fix differences on unmeasured variables.

## Interview questions

<Question q="Does a larger sample fix bias?">No. It reduces sampling variability, so a biased sample becomes a more precise estimate of the wrong number.</Question>

<Question q="When is stratified better than simple random sampling?">When strata differ on the outcome or when small groups need guaranteed representation. It usually lowers variance for the same sample size.</Question>

<Question q="Give an example of survivorship bias in a business.">Analyzing only retained customers to learn what makes them loyal. Traits seen in that group may be equally common among those who left.</Question>
