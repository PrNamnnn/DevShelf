## What it is

Diagnostic analysis explains why something happened. It starts from an observed change and narrows the possible causes until the remaining explanation can be tested.

## Why it exists

"Revenue fell 5%" prompts no specific action. "Revenue fell because Android checkout failures rose after version 8.2 in two regions" does.

## How it works

Work in this order:

1. **Confirm it's real.** Check pipelines, definition changes, tracking outages, holidays and day-of-week effects.
2. **Decompose the metric** into its factors, such as traffic × conversion × average order value.
3. **Segment.** Cut by platform, region, channel, plan and customer age to locate the change.
4. **Compute contribution.** A segment's contribution is its own change divided by the total change.
5. **Check timing and events.** Line the change up with releases, campaigns and outages.
6. **Test the explanation.** Run a holdout, an experiment, or a natural-experiment comparison.

Mix shift is a frequent trap. An overall rate can fall even when each segment improves, simply because low-rate segments grew.

## Worked example

Total orders fell from 10,000 to 9,500, a change of −500.

| Segment | Before | After | Change | Contribution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| iOS | 4,000 | 4,050 | +50 | −10% |
| Android | 5,000 | 4,400 | −600 | 120% |
| Web | 1,000 | 1,050 | +50 | −10% |

Android explains 120% of the decline, partly offset by small gains elsewhere. Next you'd split Android by app version and funnel step.

## Python

```python
import pandas as pd

orders = pd.DataFrame(
    {"before": [4_000, 5_000, 1_000], "after": [4_050, 4_400, 1_050]},
    index=["iOS", "Android", "Web"],
)
orders["change"] = orders["after"] - orders["before"]
orders["contribution"] = orders["change"] / orders["change"].sum()

assert orders["change"].sum() == -500
assert orders.loc["Android", "contribution"] == 1.2
```

## SQL

```sql
WITH o AS (
  SELECT * FROM (VALUES ('iOS', 4000, 4050), ('Android', 5000, 4400), ('Web', 1000, 1050))
    AS t(segment, before_n, after_n)
)
SELECT segment,
       after_n - before_n AS change,
       round((after_n - before_n)::numeric / sum(after_n - before_n) OVER (), 2) AS contribution
FROM o
ORDER BY contribution DESC;
-- rows: [{"segment":"Android","change":-600,"contribution":"1.20"},{"segment":"iOS","change":50,"contribution":"-0.10"},{"segment":"Web","change":50,"contribution":"-0.10"}]
```

## Excel or Power BI

Pivot Tables with drill-down and a `Difference From` calculation handle most of this. Power BI's decomposition tree and Key Influencers visuals help find candidates, and drill-through pages let users move from the summary to the detail.

## In industry

Metric review meetings use exactly this routine. Good teams keep a shared log of releases, campaigns and data incidents so that a metric change can be linked to events quickly.

## Assumptions and limitations

Segmentation finds where a change happened and doesn't by itself prove why. Slicing many ways creates false positives, and contribution percentages can look odd when positive and negative changes offset each other.

## Common mistakes

- Skipping the data-validity check.
- Stopping at the first segment that looks bad.
- Ignoring mix shift.
- Reporting a coincident event as the cause.

## Alternatives

Formal causal methods, including difference-in-differences and interrupted time series, estimate the effect of a known event. Controlled experiments are the strongest option whenever they're feasible.
