## What it is

Simple linear regression models a numeric outcome as a straight-line function of one predictor. **Ordinary least squares** chooses the line that minimizes the sum of squared residuals.

## Why it exists

Correlation states how strongly variables move together. Regression states how much the outcome changes per unit of predictor, gives predictions with uncertainty, and extends to many variables.

## How it works

<RegressionViz />

Try to reach the least-squares error by hand, then press the fit button. Squaring penalizes large misses heavily, and it gives a closed-form solution.

**Interpretation:** the slope is the expected change in y per one-unit increase in x. The intercept is the expected y at x = 0, which may have no real meaning if x never gets near zero.

## Intuition and formula

<Formula>ŷ = β₀ + β₁x</Formula>

<Formula>β₁ = cov(x, y) / var(x), β₀ = ȳ − β₁x̄</Formula>

<Formula>R² = 1 − SS_res / SS_tot</Formula>

R² is the share of variance in y explained by the model. In simple regression, it equals r².

## Worked example

Regressing revenue on ad spend (₹k) gives ŷ = 15.63 + 2.013x. Each extra ₹1k of spend is associated with ₹2.01k more revenue, with a 95% CI of 1.85 to 2.18. R² is 0.990. At ₹30k spend, the prediction is ₹76.03k. Associated is the correct word, since observational data doesn't establish that spend caused the revenue.

## Python

```python
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

spend = np.array([10, 14, 18, 22, 25, 30, 34, 38, 42, 47.0])
revenue = np.array([38, 44, 50, 62, 64, 72, 86, 90, 104, 110.0])

model = sm.OLS(revenue, sm.add_constant(spend)).fit()
intercept, slope = model.params
low, high = model.conf_int()[1]

assert (round(intercept, 2), round(slope, 3)) == (15.63, 2.013)
assert (round(low, 2), round(high, 2)) == (1.85, 2.18)
assert round(model.rsquared, 3) == 0.990
assert round(intercept + slope * 30, 2) == 76.03
```

## SQL

```sql
SELECT round(regr_intercept(r, s)::numeric, 2) AS intercept,
       round(regr_slope(r, s)::numeric, 3) AS slope,
       round(regr_r2(r, s)::numeric, 3) AS r2
FROM unnest(ARRAY[10,14,18,22,25,30,34,38,42,47]::float8[],
            ARRAY[38,44,50,62,64,72,86,90,104,110]::float8[]) AS t(s, r);
-- rows: [{"intercept":"15.63","slope":"2.013","r2":"0.990"}]
```

## Excel or Power BI

`SLOPE`, `INTERCEPT`, `RSQ` and `FORECAST.LINEAR` cover the basics, and `LINEST` returns standard errors. The Analytics pane in Power BI adds a trend line to scatter charts.

## In industry

Used for quick driver estimates, benchmarks for machine-learning models, and any setting where explanation matters as much as accuracy.

## Assumptions and limitations

Linearity, independent errors, constant variance and approximately normal errors for small-sample inference. Predicting outside the data's range is extrapolation, and it's unreliable.

## Common mistakes

- Using high R² as proof of a good or causal model.
- Interpreting a meaningless intercept.
- Extrapolating.
- Skipping residual plots.

## Alternatives

Robust regression for outliers, polynomial or spline terms for curves, and Theil-Sen estimators for a median-based slope.

## Interview questions

<Question q="What does OLS minimize?">The sum of squared differences between observed and fitted values.</Question>

<Question q="Can a high R² model be bad?">Yes. It may violate assumptions, be overfit, reflect a shared trend, or fail on new data.</Question>
