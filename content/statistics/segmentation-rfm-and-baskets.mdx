## What it is

**Segmentation** divides customers into groups that behave differently and should be treated differently. **RFM** scores each customer on Recency, Frequency and Monetary value. **Market basket analysis** finds items purchased together.

## Why it exists

Treating every customer identically wastes money. RFM is cheap, needs only transaction data, and can be explained to a marketing team in one sentence.

## How it works

For RFM, compute days since last purchase, number of purchases and total spend per customer. Rank each into quantiles, then combine scores into named segments. A high recency score means a recent purchase, so its ordering is reversed.

For association rules on A → B:

| Measure | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Support | Share of all baskets with both A and B |
| Confidence | Of baskets with A, the share that also contain B |
| Lift | Confidence ÷ overall share of baskets with B |

Lift above 1 means they co-occur more than chance would imply.

## Intuition and formula

<Formula>lift(A → B) = P(A and B) / (P(A) · P(B))</Formula>

## Worked example

From 1,000 baskets, 120 contain pasta, 60 contain sauce and 40 contain both. Support is 4%. Confidence for pasta → sauce is 33.3%. Baseline sauce rate is 6%, so lift is 5.56. Pasta buyers are over five times more likely to buy sauce.

## Python

```python
import pandas as pd

tx = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer": ["a", "a", "b", "c", "c", "c", "d"],
    "days_ago": [5, 40, 90, 2, 10, 30, 200],
    "amount": [100, 50, 80, 200, 150, 120, 40],
})
rfm = tx.groupby("customer").agg(
    recency=("days_ago", "min"), frequency=("days_ago", "size"), monetary=("amount", "sum")
)
rfm["r_score"] = pd.qcut(rfm["recency"], 2, labels=[2, 1]).astype(int)
assert rfm.loc["c", "monetary"] == 470 and rfm.loc["c", "r_score"] == 2

baskets, a, b, both = 1_000, 120, 60, 40
lift = (both / a) / (b / baskets)
assert round(lift, 2) == 5.56
```

## SQL

```sql
WITH tx AS (
  SELECT * FROM (VALUES ('a', 5, 100), ('a', 40, 50), ('b', 90, 80), ('c', 2, 200), ('c', 10, 150))
    AS t(customer, days_ago, amount)
)
SELECT customer, min(days_ago) AS recency, count(*) AS frequency, sum(amount) AS monetary,
       ntile(2) OVER (ORDER BY min(days_ago) DESC) AS r_score
FROM tx
GROUP BY customer
ORDER BY customer;
-- rows: [{"customer":"a","recency":5,"frequency":2,"monetary":150,"r_score":1},{"customer":"b","recency":90,"frequency":1,"monetary":80,"r_score":1},{"customer":"c","recency":2,"frequency":2,"monetary":350,"r_score":2}]
```

## Excel or Power BI

Use `PERCENTRANK.INC` or `NTILE`-style formulas to score RFM in Excel, and Pivot Tables to size segments. In DAX, build calculated tables or measures with `RANKX` and a slicer per segment.

## In industry

Retailers use RFM to prioritize retention campaigns. Basket results drive bundles, placement and "frequently bought together" modules.

## Assumptions and limitations

RFM ignores margin, returns and acquisition channel unless they're added. Rule mining finds co-occurrence, not cause. Low-support rules with high lift are often noise, so set minimum support.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to reverse recency's scoring direction.
- Ranking rules only by confidence, which favours very popular items.
- Building segments no team can act upon.
- Calculating RFM on a period that doesn't match purchase cycles.

## Alternatives

K-means or Gaussian mixtures find segments from many behavioural features. Collaborative filtering and sequence models power personalized recommendations at scale.

## Interview questions

<Question q="Why does lift matter more than confidence?">Confidence can be high simply because the consequent is popular. Lift compares against that base rate.</Question>

<Question q="How would you validate a segmentation?">Check stability over time, whether segments differ on outcomes that matter, whether they can be reached, and whether treating them differently improves a measured result.</Question>
