## What it is

**Covariance** measures whether two variables move together, in units that are hard to interpret. **Pearson's r** rescales covariance to between −1 and 1, measuring linear association. **Spearman's ρ** applies Pearson to ranks, measuring monotonic association. **Causation** means that changing one variable would change the other.

## Why it exists

Correlation finds promising relationships cheaply. It's also the most misused number in analytics, because people instinctively read association as cause.

## How it works

Pearson needs numeric variables and is sensitive to outliers. Spearman works for ordinal data or curved but monotonic trends. A correlation of 0 means no linear relationship, and a perfect parabola has r near 0.

Association without causation usually has one of these causes:

| Cause | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Confounding | Ice cream sales and drownings both follow summer |
| Reverse causation | Support tickets rise before cancellations, partly because of bugs |
| Selection | Conditioning on an outcome creates false links |
| Chance | Searching many pairs finds something |

Causal claims need randomization, or a credible design and explicit assumptions.

## Intuition and formula

<Formula>cov(x, y) = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) / (n − 1)</Formula>

<Formula>r = cov(x, y) / (sₓ s_y)</Formula>

## Worked example

Monthly ad spend and revenue for 10 months give a covariance of 304.67, Pearson's r = 0.995 and Spearman's ρ = 1.0. Very strong, but the budget was likely raised in seasons with more demand. A geo-holdout test would estimate what the ads actually caused.

## Python

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

spend = np.array([10, 14, 18, 22, 25, 30, 34, 38, 42, 47.0])
revenue = np.array([38, 44, 50, 62, 64, 72, 86, 90, 104, 110.0])

assert round(np.cov(spend, revenue)[0, 1], 2) == 304.67
assert round(stats.pearsonr(spend, revenue).statistic, 3) == 0.995
assert round(stats.spearmanr(spend, revenue).statistic, 6) == 1

x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 101)
assert abs(np.corrcoef(x, x**2)[0, 1]) < 1e-9
```

## SQL

```sql
SELECT round(covar_samp(s, r)::numeric, 2) AS cov, round(corr(s, r)::numeric, 3) AS r
FROM unnest(ARRAY[10,14,18,22,25,30,34,38,42,47]::float8[],
            ARRAY[38,44,50,62,64,72,86,90,104,110]::float8[]) AS t(s, r);
-- rows: [{"cov":"304.67","r":"0.995"}]
```

## Excel or Power BI

`CORREL(a, b)` for Pearson, `COVARIANCE.S(a, b)` for covariance, and `CORREL` on columns of `RANK.AVG` for Spearman. Power BI offers a correlation quick measure, or calculate one with DAX iterators.

## In industry

Correlation matrices guide feature review, and marketing mix models and experiments are how companies move from correlation toward causal claims.

## Assumptions and limitations

Pearson's significance test assumes approximately bivariate normal data. Outliers can create or destroy a correlation. Correlations in time series are often spurious because both series have trends.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a high r as causal.
- Calling r near 0 "no relationship" without a plot.
- Correlating two trending time series.
- Reporting Pearson on heavily skewed values.

## Alternatives

Kendall's τ for small samples with many ties, mutual information for general dependence, and causal designs like difference-in-differences and instrumental variables.

## Interview questions

<Question q="Pearson vs Spearman?">Pearson measures linear association on raw values. Spearman measures monotonic association on ranks, and is more robust to outliers.</Question>

<Question q="How would you test whether a marketing channel causes sales?">Randomize exposure by region or user through a holdout or geo experiment, and compare outcomes.</Question>
