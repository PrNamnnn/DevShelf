## What it is

Non-parametric tests make weaker distributional assumptions, typically by analyzing ranks.

| Test | Replaces | Design | H₀ (informal) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mann-Whitney U | Independent t | 2 independent groups | Neither group tends to have larger values |
| Wilcoxon signed-rank | Paired t | 2 paired samples | Differences are symmetric around 0 |
| Kruskal-Wallis | One-way ANOVA | 3+ independent groups | Same distribution across groups |
| Friedman | Repeated ANOVA | 3+ repeated conditions | Same ranks across conditions |
| Kolmogorov-Smirnov | | 2 samples or a reference | Same distribution |

## Why it exists

Small samples with heavy skew, outliers or ordinal scales break the assumptions behind t-tests. Ranks limit how much any one extreme value can influence the outcome.

## How it works

**When to use:** ordinal outcomes, or small samples with strong non-normality. **Assumptions:** independence (or paired independence), with some tests requiring similar shapes for a median interpretation.

**P-value:** the chance of a rank pattern this extreme under H₀. **Business meaning:** "customers in B tend to spend more than in A", which isn't a claim about average revenue.

Mann-Whitney's statistic counts, across all pairs, how often one group beats the other. U ÷ (n₁n₂) estimates P(X > Y) and is a useful effect size. KS is sensitive to any distribution difference, including spread or shape, and it's used in drift detection.

## Intuition and formula

<Formula>U₁ = R₁ − n₁(n₁ + 1) / 2</Formula>

R₁ is the sum of ranks of group 1 in the combined data.

## Worked example

Two groups of seven purchase values each, both with one extreme value: A = 12, 15, 14, 10, 39, 13, 11 and B = 22, 25, 19, 27, 24, 120, 21. U = 6, p = 0.017. Value 120 counts simply as the highest rank. 6 ÷ 49 = 12% of pairs have A above B, so B tends to be larger.

Paired scores (8 agents): Wilcoxon W = 1, p = 0.016. Three groups: Kruskal-Wallis H = 9.69, p = 0.0079. Repeated ratings: Friedman χ² = 10.18, p = 0.0062.

## Python

```python
from scipy import stats

a = [12, 15, 14, 10, 39, 13, 11]
b = [22, 25, 19, 27, 24, 120, 21]
mw = stats.mannwhitneyu(a, b)
assert (mw.statistic, round(mw.pvalue, 3)) == (6.0, 0.017)

before = [62, 70, 55, 68, 74, 59, 66, 71]
after = [66, 75, 58, 70, 80, 63, 65, 78]
assert round(stats.wilcoxon(after, before).pvalue, 3) == 0.016

kw = stats.kruskal([23, 25, 21, 24, 26], [28, 30, 27, 29, 31], [22, 24, 23, 25, 21])
assert round(kw.statistic, 2) == 9.69

fr = stats.friedmanchisquare([5, 6, 5, 7, 6, 5], [7, 8, 6, 8, 7, 7], [6, 7, 6, 8, 8, 6])
assert round(fr.statistic, 2) == 10.18

ks = stats.ks_2samp(a, b)
assert 0 <= ks.pvalue <= 1
```

## SQL

```sql
WITH d(g, x) AS (
  SELECT 'a', v FROM unnest(ARRAY[12,15,14,10,39,13,11]) AS v
  UNION ALL SELECT 'b', v FROM unnest(ARRAY[22,25,19,27,24,120,21]) AS v
), r AS (SELECT g, rank() OVER (ORDER BY x) AS rnk FROM d)
SELECT sum(rnk) - 7 * 8 / 2 AS u FROM r WHERE g = 'a';
-- rows: [{"u":"6"}]
```

`rank()` gives tied values the same low rank. A correct test with ties needs the average rank.

## Excel or Power BI

`RANK.AVG` produces proper tied ranks, and the U statistic can then be calculated with a `SUMIF`. Excel doesn't provide these tests directly.

## In industry

Useful for ratings, small pilot programs and heavy-tailed operational data. Two-sample KS tests are a common way to monitor feature drift.

## Assumptions and limitations

They aren't assumption-free. Mann-Whitney is not generally a test of medians unless the two distributions share a shape. Massive samples will flag tiny differences. Power is lower than parametric tests when normality holds, though often not by much.

## Common mistakes

- Reporting Mann-Whitney as a median comparison.
- Using it when the business decision depends on means.
- Using ordinary rank tests on paired data.
- Ignoring ties in small samples.

## Alternatives

Permutation and bootstrap methods for many statistics, robust trimmed-mean tests, and transformations such as log followed by parametric tests.

## Interview questions

<Question q="Does Mann-Whitney compare medians?">Only under the additional assumption that the two distributions have the same shape. In general it tests whether one group tends to produce larger values.</Question>

<Question q="When would you use KS?">To compare entire distributions, such as checking whether production data has drifted from training data.</Question>
