## What it is

A **sampling distribution** is the distribution of a statistic across repeated samples. The **Law of Large Numbers** says the sample mean converges to the true mean as n grows. The **Central Limit Theorem** says that, for independent observations with finite variance, the distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution, whatever the original shape.

## Why it exists

Because of the CLT, you can make probability statements about an average without knowing the population's distribution. It's the main reason z-tests and t-tests work on skewed data at sufficient scale.

## Intuition and formula

<Formula>SE(x̄) = σ / √n</Formula>

Halving the standard error requires four times the data. Averaging lets high and low values cancel, which is why means vary less than individual observations.

## Worked example

Order values average ₹1,000 with an SD of ₹1,000, and are strongly skewed. For samples of 36 orders the standard error is about ₹167, so the sample mean is approximately normal around ₹1,000. The probability a sample mean exceeds ₹1,500 is P(Z > 3) = 0.13%, while plenty of individual orders exceed that amount.

## Python

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
means = rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=(50_000, 36)).mean(axis=1)

assert abs(means.mean() - 1.0) < 0.01
assert abs(means.std(ddof=1) - 1 / 6) < 0.005

upper = np.mean(means > 1.5)
assert 0.001 < upper < 0.008
```

The simulated tail is a little heavier than the normal figure of 0.13%, because at n = 36 skew hasn't completely disappeared.

## In industry

Experiment platforms apply normal approximations to mean metrics across millions of users. Monitoring systems likewise use averages over a window to reduce noise.

## Assumptions and limitations

The CLT needs independence and finite variance. Convergence is slow for very skewed or heavy-tailed data: revenue with rare huge orders may need far more than the often-quoted 30 observations. Extreme percentiles aren't covered by the same result.

## Common mistakes

- Believing the data itself becomes normal as n grows.
- Applying "n ≥ 30" as a universal rule.
- Ignoring clustering, such as repeated sessions from each user.
- Thinking that a large sample corrects bias.

## Alternatives

Bootstrapping approximates the sampling distribution by resampling from the data, and it works for medians and other statistics that have no simple formula.
