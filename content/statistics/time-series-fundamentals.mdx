## What it is

A time series is a sequence of observations ordered in time. Its usual components are:

| Component | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trend | Long-term direction | Rising subscribers |
| Seasonality | Fixed-period pattern | Weekend orders |
| Cycle | Irregular longer swings | Business cycle |
| Noise | Random remainder | Daily fluctuation |

## Why it exists

Observations close in time are correlated, which breaks the independence behind most classical statistics. Time is also the basis of forecasting and of any "is this change unusual?" question.

## How it works

Moving averages smooth noise. A window equal to the seasonal period removes the seasonal pattern. A trailing window suits forecasting, since a centered window uses future values.

**Additive** decomposition is y = T + S + R and fits constant seasonal swings. **Multiplicative** decomposition is y = T × S × R and fits swings that grow with the level. Logging a multiplicative series makes it additive.

## Intuition and formula

<Formula>MAₜ = (yₜ + yₜ₋₁ + … + yₜ₋ₖ₊₁) / k</Formula>

## Worked example

Ten years of simulated monthly sales have a trend of +1.5 per month, an annual sine pattern and noise. Decomposition recovers monthly seasonal effects, ranging from about −27 in October to +25 in April. Year-over-year comparisons avoid seasonal effects without fitting anything, which explains why business reports use them.

## Python

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
n = 120
index = pd.date_range("2016-01-01", periods=n, freq="MS")
y = pd.Series(200 + 1.5 * np.arange(n) + 25 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * np.arange(n) / 12) + rng.normal(0, 6, n), index=index)

parts = seasonal_decompose(y, period=12)
seasonal = parts.seasonal.iloc[:12]
assert seasonal.idxmin().month == 10
assert seasonal.idxmax().month == 4
assert abs(seasonal.sum()) < 1e-6

smooth = y.rolling(12).mean()
assert smooth.isna().sum() == 11
```

## SQL

```sql
SELECT day, round(avg(orders) OVER (ORDER BY day ROWS BETWEEN 2 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW), 1) AS ma3
FROM (VALUES (1, 10), (2, 20), (3, 30), (4, 40)) AS t(day, orders)
ORDER BY day DESC
LIMIT 1;
-- rows: [{"day":4,"ma3":"30.0"}]
```

## Excel or Power BI

Use `AVERAGE` with a rolling range, or a line chart's Moving Average trendline. In DAX, `DATESINPERIOD` builds rolling windows and `SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR` handles year-over-year comparison.

## In industry

Business reviews look at week-over-week and year-over-year figures, and demand planning relies on decomposed patterns.

## Assumptions and limitations

Classical decomposition assumes a stable seasonal shape and loses values at the edges. Holidays that move between dates need separate handling.

## Common mistakes

- Random train/test splits on time data.
- Comparing December with November and calling it growth.
- Using centered windows in a real-time feature.
- Missing that a series has multiple seasonalities.

## Alternatives

STL decomposition handles changing seasonality and is robust to outliers. Prophet-style models or regression with calendar features handle holidays.

## Interview questions

<Question q="Additive or multiplicative seasonality?">Additive if seasonal swings are roughly constant, multiplicative if they scale with the level.</Question>

<Question q="Why not randomly split a time series?">It leaks future information into training and overstates accuracy.</Question>
