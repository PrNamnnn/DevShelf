## What it is

| | H₀ true | H₀ false |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reject H₀ | **Type I error** (false positive), rate α | Correct, rate = power |
| Don't reject | Correct, rate 1 − α | **Type II error** (false negative), rate β |

**Power** is 1 − β, the chance of detecting an effect that really exists. **Effect size** measures how large a difference is, on a standardized or business scale.

## Why it exists

An underpowered study wastes time because even real effects are likely to be missed. And when an underpowered study does reach significance, its estimated effect tends to be exaggerated, a problem known as the winner's curse.

## How it works

Four quantities are linked: α, power, sample size and effect size. Fix three and the fourth follows. Use the sliders below and see power rise with a larger n, a larger effect or a looser α.

<HypothesisViz />

Common standardized measures include Cohen's d for means, odds ratio or risk difference for proportions, Cramér's V for tables, and η² for ANOVA. Cohen's benchmarks of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 are generic conventions. A business-specific effect, like ₹ per user, is usually more meaningful.

## Intuition and formula

<Formula>d = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / s_pooled</Formula>

## Worked example

For a two-group t-test detecting d = 0.3 at α = 0.05 with 80% power, you need about 176 per group. With only 100 per group, power is 56%, so nearly half of such real effects would go undetected.

Weighing the errors is a business call. Falsely shipping a harmful pricing change may be far worse than missing a small improvement, which argues for a stricter α. Screening candidates for further research would justify a looser one.

## Python

```python
from statsmodels.stats.power import TTestIndPower

analysis = TTestIndPower()
n = analysis.solve_power(effect_size=0.3, alpha=0.05, power=0.8)
power = analysis.power(effect_size=0.3, nobs1=100, alpha=0.05)

assert round(n) == 175
assert round(power, 2) == 0.56
```

Round n up in practice: the solution is 175.4, so plan for 176.

## Excel or Power BI

Excel has no native power function. Cohen's d can be computed from `AVERAGE` and `STDEV.S`, and power can be built from `NORM.S.DIST`.

## In industry

Teams compute power before launching experiments, to avoid running tests that can't succeed. Post-launch, they report whether the interval excludes the minimum effect they cared about.

## Assumptions and limitations

Power calculations rely on assumed variance and effect size. Power computed after a study from the observed effect adds no information beyond the p-value, so it shouldn't be used to explain a null result.

## Common mistakes

- Treating statistical and practical significance as the same.
- Stopping a test early because it's significant.
- Powering for an unrealistically large effect.
- Quoting Cohen's labels with no domain context.

## Alternatives

Bayesian decision analysis weighs expected loss rather than error rates. Simulation estimates power for complex designs or metrics.

## Interview questions

<Question q="What would you trade off when choosing alpha?">A lower α reduces false positives but requires more data or reduces power. The choice depends on the relative cost of each error.</Question>

<Question q="Why is post-hoc power not useful?">It's a direct function of the observed p-value, so it adds no new information.</Question>
