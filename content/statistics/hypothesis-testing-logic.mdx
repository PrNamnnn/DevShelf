## What it is

A hypothesis test measures how compatible the data is with a specific default claim, called the **null hypothesis** (H₀). The **alternative** (H₁) states what you'd conclude instead.

## Why it exists

Random variation makes groups differ even when nothing real has changed. Testing provides a controlled rule for deciding when a difference is too large to ignore, with a known false-positive rate.

A two-tailed test detects change in either direction. A one-tailed test puts all of α in one direction, so it's only justifiable when the opposite result would lead to the same action, and the direction was fixed beforehand. In product experiments, two-tailed is the normal choice, since harm matters.

**A p-value is not** the probability that H₀ is true, the probability that the result happened by chance alone, or a measure of effect size. Failing to reject also doesn't prove H₀.

## Intuition and formula

<Formula>test statistic = (observed − expected under H₀) / standard error</Formula>

## Worked example

A site's historical conversion is 5%. After a redesign, 130 of 2,000 visitors convert (6.5%). Under H₀: p = 0.05, the standard error is √(0.05 · 0.95 / 2000) = 0.00487, so z = 3.08 and the two-sided p-value is 0.0021. Evidence is strong, but this comparison isn't randomized, so a seasonal change might also explain it.

## Python

```python
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest

z, p = proportions_ztest(count=130, nobs=2_000, value=0.05, prop_var=0.05)
assert round(z, 2) == 3.08
assert round(p, 4) == 0.0021
```

## Excel or Power BI

`NORM.S.DIST(z, TRUE)` gives a lower-tail probability, so a two-sided p-value is `=2*(1-NORM.S.DIST(ABS(z),TRUE))`. `T.TEST` directly returns a p-value for two ranges.

## In industry

Most statistical decisions in analytics follow this framework, and strong teams report effects and intervals with the p-value.

## Assumptions and limitations

Every test assumes a data-generating model, usually including independence. With huge samples, trivial differences become significant, and with small samples, real effects may not be. A threshold of 0.05 is a convention, not a scientific law.

## Common mistakes

- Saying p = 0.03 means a 97% chance the variant works.
- Claiming "no effect" from a non-significant result.
- Choosing one-tailed after seeing the direction.
- Running many tests and only reporting the significant ones.

## Alternatives

Confidence intervals show the range of effects consistent with the data. Bayesian analysis yields a posterior probability. Equivalence tests can provide evidence that an effect is negligible.
