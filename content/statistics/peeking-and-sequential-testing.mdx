## What it is

**Peeking** means checking results repeatedly and stopping as soon as significance appears. **Sequential testing** is a class of methods designed to permit valid early decisions.

## Why it exists

A p-value assumes the sample size was fixed in advance. With repeated looks, a random walk has many chances to cross the threshold, so the real false-positive rate climbs well above α.

## How it works

Three common solutions are:

| Method | Idea |
| --- | --- |
| Group sequential | A few planned looks with stricter thresholds, such as O'Brien-Fleming |
| Alpha spending | Spends α over time, permitting flexible look times |
| Always-valid inference | Confidence sequences that hold at every moment |

Early boundaries are strict, so a test ends early only with very strong evidence, and the final threshold stays near 0.05.

Peeking for **safety** is fine as long as stopping only follows severe harm, with pre-agreed rules, and doesn't decide a success.

## Worked example

A simulation of 4,000 A/A tests has no true effect. Each test looks every 100 users until 2,000, 20 looks in all, using a threshold of |z| > 1.96. Roughly 25% of tests falsely reach significance at some look, five times the intended 5%.

## Python

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
trials, false_positives = 2_000, 0
looks = np.arange(100, 2_001, 100)

for _ in range(trials):
    a, b = rng.normal(0, 1, (2, 2_000)).cumsum(axis=1)
    z = (b[looks - 1] - a[looks - 1]) / looks / np.sqrt(2 / looks)
    false_positives += np.any(np.abs(z) > 1.96)

rate = false_positives / trials
assert 0.20 < rate < 0.30
```

## In industry

Commercial experiment platforms frequently provide sequential confidence intervals, since people will look at dashboards regardless of what they're told.

## Assumptions and limitations

Sequential procedures pay for flexibility in the form of wider intervals or slightly larger maximum samples. Early results can be exaggerated, so estimates may need correction, and stopping early misses a full weekly cycle.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a dashboard's fixed-horizon p-value as valid at any moment.
- Extending a test because it's "almost significant".
- Stopping at first significance.
- Skipping a guardrail check because the primary goal has already passed.

## Alternatives

Fix the horizon and don't act before it. Bayesian methods are also sensitive to stopping rules in terms of frequentist error, though they're often claimed not to be.
