## What it is

A continuous random variable is described by a **probability density function**. Probability is the area under the curve across a range, and the chance of any exact value is zero.

## Why it exists

Durations, amounts and measurement errors are modelled more naturally as continuous quantities. The normal distribution also appears through the Central Limit Theorem when averages are involved.

## How it works

<DistributionExplorer only={["normal", "uniform", "exponential"]} title="Continuous distributions" />

| Distribution | Models | Mean | Variance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Uniform(a, b) | Equally likely values in a range | (a + b)/2 | (b − a)²/12 |
| Exponential(λ) | Time between independent events | 1/λ | 1/λ² |
| Normal(μ, σ) | Sums of many small effects | μ | σ² |

A density value can exceed 1, since only area must total 1. The exponential distribution is **memoryless**: having waited already doesn't change the distribution of the remaining wait. Standardizing with z = (x − μ)/σ turns any normal variable into the standard normal.

## Intuition and formula

<Formula>f(x) = (1 / (σ√(2π))) · exp(−(x − μ)² / (2σ²))</Formula>

About 68% of a normal distribution lies within 1σ, 95% within 1.96σ, and 99.7% within 3σ.

## Worked example

Daily orders are approximately normal with mean 500 and SD 80. For 600 orders, z = 1.25, so the chance of exceeding 600 is 10.6%. If customers arrive at 2 per minute, the chance of a gap longer than 2 minutes is e⁻⁴ = 1.8%.

## Python

```python
from scipy import stats

assert round(stats.norm.sf(600, loc=500, scale=80), 3) == 0.106
assert round(stats.norm.cdf(1.96), 3) == 0.975
assert round(stats.expon.sf(2, scale=1 / 2), 4) == 0.0183
```

<Callout type="warning">SciPy's exponential distribution uses `scale`, which is 1/λ, instead of the rate λ.</Callout>

## Excel or Power BI

`NORM.DIST(x, mean, sd, TRUE)` gives a cumulative probability, `NORM.INV(p, mean, sd)` finds a quantile, and `NORM.S.INV(0.975)` returns 1.96. `EXPON.DIST` takes the rate λ.

## In industry

Process control limits, confidence intervals and many tests depend on normal approximations. Exponential waiting times are the basis of simple queueing models, used in staffing and capacity planning.

## Assumptions and limitations

Few raw business metrics are normal. Tails are often heavier, so a normal model underestimates extreme events. Rates vary in time, so exponential waits typically only apply within short, stable windows.

## Common mistakes

- Treating density height as probability.
- Assuming a variable is normal because it's continuous.
- Mixing up rate and scale.
- Applying the 68-95-99.7 rule to skewed data.

## Alternatives

Log-normal models fit positive skewed amounts, Weibull models flexible waiting times, and empirical distributions or a bootstrap avoid parametric assumptions.
