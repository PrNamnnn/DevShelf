## What it is

A **random variable** assigns a number to each outcome of a random process. It's **discrete** if it takes countable values, described by a probability mass function, and **continuous** if it takes values in a range, described by a density.

## Why it exists

Named distributions are reusable models. Once a process matches one, you get probabilities, expected values and the basis for tests with no need to simulate.

## How it works

<DistributionExplorer only={["bernoulli", "binomial", "geometric", "poisson"]} title="Discrete distributions" />

| Distribution | Models | Mean | Variance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bernoulli(p) | One yes/no trial | p | p(1 − p) |
| Binomial(n, p) | Successes in n independent trials | np | np(1 − p) |
| Geometric(p) | Trials until first success | 1/p | (1 − p)/p² |
| Poisson(λ) | Events in a fixed interval | λ | λ |

Expected value is the long-run average, a weighted sum E[X] = Σ x · P(x). A Poisson distribution's variance equals its mean, so if real counts vary much more than that, they're **overdispersed**, and a Poisson model will be too confident.

## Intuition and formula

<Formula>P(X = k) = C(n, k) pᵏ (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ</Formula>

C(n, k) counts the ways to place k successes among n trials.

<Formula>P(X = k) = λᵏ e^(−λ) / k!</Formula>

## Worked example

With a 10% email-click rate and 20 recipients, the probability of exactly 3 clicks is 19.0%, and of 5 or more is 4.3%. A support queue averaging 3 tickets an hour has a 5.0% chance of zero tickets and a 3.4% chance of more than 6. With a 25% chance of closing a deal per call, the probability the first close happens on the third call is 0.75² × 0.25 = 14.1%.

## Python

```python
from scipy import stats

assert round(stats.binom.pmf(3, 20, 0.1), 3) == 0.190
assert round(stats.binom.sf(4, 20, 0.1), 3) == 0.043
assert round(stats.poisson.pmf(0, 3), 3) == 0.050
assert round(stats.poisson.sf(6, 3), 3) == 0.034
assert round(stats.geom.pmf(3, 0.25), 4) == 0.1406
```

## Excel or Power BI

Excel provides `BINOM.DIST(k, n, p, FALSE)`, `POISSON.DIST(k, mean, FALSE)` and `NEGBINOM.DIST` for waiting-time problems. Use `TRUE` for cumulative probability.

## In industry

Binomial models underlie conversion analysis. Poisson models help with staffing, inventory and rare-event rates. Geometric thinking is useful for "how many attempts until success" in sales and hiring funnels.

## Assumptions and limitations

Binomial assumes independent trials with constant p, which breaks when one customer visits many times. Poisson assumes independent events at a constant rate, which daily traffic cycles violate unless modelled.

## Common mistakes

- Using a binomial model on repeat visits by the same user.
- Ignoring overdispersion in counts.
- Mixing up `pmf` with `cdf` or `sf`.
- Forgetting that `sf(k)` means strictly greater than k.

## Alternatives

Negative binomial models handle overdispersed counts. Beta-binomial models handle varying conversion rates. Bootstrapping avoids assuming a distribution altogether.

## Interview questions

<Question q="When do you use Poisson instead of binomial?">When counting events in time or space without a fixed number of trials. A binomial with large n and small p is approximately Poisson with λ = np.</Question>

<Question q="What is overdispersion?">Variance larger than the model expects, typically because the rate differs between units or events cluster. It causes standard errors to be understated.</Question>
